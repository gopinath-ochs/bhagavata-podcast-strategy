Two March Figures Are Not Safely Comparable

The March report also recorded 3.7% CTR and 9:55 average view duration, but not how either was filtered — and neither reconciles with its own stated totals: 1,600 watch hours across 45,475 views implies an average view duration of 2:07, not 9:55. Whatever filter produced 9:55 is unrecorded.

This brief therefore makes no claim of change in CTR or view duration against March. Where those metrics are analysed below, the comparison is Season 1 against Season 2 within a single export — which is like-for-like and reliable. The six lifetime totals in the table above are directly comparable and unaffected.

Spotify

Resolved: the Spotify Plays and Audience Figures Do Not Measure an Audience

Spotify for Creators reports 69,044 plays and an audience of 49,649 all time. The Audience tab settles what those numbers are. Over the full period from 1 January 2025 to 17 August 2026 it breaks the audience down as:

New 49,625  ·  Returning 0

Zero returning listeners across twenty months. A genuine listening base of fifty thousand people would generate substantial repeat listening; a figure with no returning component is a reach or impression count, not an audience. It is also irreconcilable with the same dashboard's daily chart of 20–70 plays per day, and with Buzzsprout attributing only 15% of downloads to Spotify.

Conclusion: do not use 69,044 or 49,649 as audience or listenership figures — not externally, and not in funding reports. Spotify's own notice records that it changed how plays are counted on 11 June 2026, which likely bears on the discontinuity. For Spotify listening, Buzzsprout's Spotify share (15% of downloads, 154 on recent episodes) and the dashboard's stated normal range of 24–28 plays in an episode's first seven days are the reliable measures. Both are consistent with each other; neither is consistent with 69,044.

Spotify's follower count was not present in any export supplied and remains unknown. It stood at 252 in March.

The Early Drop-Off Does Not Explain the Retention Gap

Both episodes retain exactly 53% of viewers at the 0:30 mark — the best-retaining episode on the channel and the worst lose an identical share in the first thirty seconds. Whatever separates 31.3% from 11.3% happens after the opening, not during it.

This matters because the March report attributed the retention problem substantially to the Sanskrit opening prayer deterring newcomers. The data does not support that as the explanation for variance between episodes: roughly 47% leave in the first half-minute regardless of how well the episode goes on to perform. Improving the open may raise the floor for everything, but it is not what distinguishes a strong episode from a weak one.

Note also that 2.5 accumulated 147.2 watch hours against a typical range of 40–70 for comparable videos — more total watch time than any other Season 2 episode — while its average view ran 5:22 below usual. Exceptional reach, poor holding.

Ten

Diagnosing the Data

Nine things the numbers show. Each follows from figures above; none extends into recommendation.

1. Reach and subscriber growth come from different places

The channel's view count is a Shorts number and its subscriber count is an episode number. 85 Shorts delivered 53 subscribers; episode 1.1 alone delivered 122. YouTube's own traffic attribution says the same thing from the other direction: the Shorts feed supplies 68.6% of views and 2.9% of watch time, at an average view of 16 seconds.

Any metric that blends the two formats — total views, channel-wide CTR, channel-wide view duration — describes neither and moves mainly with the ratio between them. This is why March's 1% conversion figure misled: arithmetically correct, but averaging two populations that differ by a factor of 23. Measured separately, full episodes convert at 2.35%, near the low end of the 3–5% range typical for niche educational channels rather than far below it.

2. Click-through improved, retention did not

Season 2 lifts mean CTR from 4.33% to 5.49% and median from 4.22% to 5.51% — consistent across both measures, so not an artefact of one outlier. Over the same rewrite, mean retention moved from 21.7% to 19.9% and median from 22.1% to 20.9%: flat, marginally down. The repackaging changed behaviour at the thumbnail and did not change behaviour after the click.

3. The early drop-off is not what separates good episodes from bad ones

The best-retaining episode on the channel (1.5, 31.3%) and the worst (2.5, 11.3%) both retain exactly 53% of viewers at 0:30. Close to half of all viewers leave in the first thirty seconds of every episode, regardless of how the episode goes on to perform.

The March report attributed the retention problem substantially to the Sanskrit opening prayer deterring newcomers. As an account of why episodes differ from each other, the data rejects that: the opening loses the same share either way. The divergence occurs later in the episode.

4. Episode 2.5 is an outlier in two directions at once

2.5 has the most Season 2 views (1,306), the most subscribers (34), the highest impressions after 1.1 (21,085), 147.2 watch hours against a typical range of 40–70 — and the worst retention on the channel at 11.3%, an average view of 6:45 against a 60-minute episode.

High reach with unusually shallow engagement is the signature of traffic arriving from outside the established audience. In the July–August window it supplied 30% of views and 37% of subscribers; remove it and the period is close to flat.

5. Playlists are the best traffic on the channel and the least used

Playlist traffic accounts for 1.9% of views but 12.6% of all watch time, with an average view duration of 17:49 and 29.2% of the episode watched — the highest of any source, and roughly half again the channel's overall episode retention. Its CTR of 4.6% is second only to Browse.

It receives 17,349 impressions. Browse receives 91,071 and Suggested 81,267. The highest-quality route into the catalogue is the one being surfaced least.

6. Discovery and loyalty are carried by two different surfaces

Browse features — the homepage and subscription feed, i.e. the existing audience — produces 27.6% of watch time from 8.9% of views at the channel's best CTR (4.9%). Suggested videos, the cold-discovery surface, produces a comparable 81,267 impressions but converts at only 2.6%, the weakest of any major source.

Existing viewers click readily; strangers do not. Since both surfaces show the same thumbnails, the difference is prior relationship rather than packaging alone — but 2.6% on 81,000 impressions is where the largest absolute gain sits.

7. Two distribution mechanisms are effectively switched off

End screens have produced 4 views in the channel's lifetime, and episode 1.5's end-screen click rate is 0.0% against a 0.9% channel average. Search accounts for 3.7% of views at a 3.0% CTR across 31,017 impressions, and 90.4% of views use no subtitles. Neither the internal routing mechanism nor text-based discovery is doing measurable work.

External traffic — everything off-platform, including any sharing through community networks — is 2.2% of views.

8. The audio audience behaves nothing like the video audience

Apple listeners consume 42–82% of an episode. YouTube viewers watch about 20%. Apple listener counts sit between 40 and 65 for every episode measured, where YouTube views vary twentyfold. 81% of Apple listening comes from followers.

This is the strongest evidence in the dataset that the hour-long format is not itself the problem. The same conversation, unedited, holds an audio audience two to four times more completely than a video one. What differs is the context of consumption and the composition of who arrives.

Against that, Apple consumption has slid through Season 2 — 82% on 2.1 down to 42% on 2.6. Recency may account for part of it; the consistency across six consecutive releases suggests not all of it.

9. There is no single audience

Spotify's audience is 59.1% Indian, with 45–59 its largest age band and a third female. YouTube is 27.9% US and 7.0% Indian, with 25–34 its largest band and 14% female. Apple is the most US-concentrated of the three. Buzzsprout overall reads US 56%, India 14%.

These are four different populations with different geography, age and gender profiles. Statements about "the audience" that do not name a platform are averaging across all four, and the average describes none of them.

10. Retention varies enough to rule out format as the cause

Retention across 26 episodes ranges from 11.3% to 31.3% — nearly a threefold spread on episodes of near-identical length, structure and production. Episode 1.5 holds viewers for 17:56 and 1.8 for 17:31; 2.5 holds them for 6:45. If the hour-long conversational format were the ceiling, that spread would be narrow. Combined with Apple's 42–82%, the constraint sits in the content and its context, not in the length.
