Two March Figures Are Not Safely Comparable

The March report also recorded 3.7% CTR and 9:55 average view duration, but not how either was filtered — and neither reconciles with its own stated totals: 1,600 watch hours across 45,475 views implies an average view duration of 2:07, not 9:55. Whatever filter produced 9:55 is unrecorded.

This brief therefore makes no claim of change in CTR or view duration against March. Where those metrics are analysed below, the comparison is Season 1 against Season 2 within a single export — which is like-for-like and reliable. The six lifetime totals in the table above are directly comparable and unaffected.

Spotify

These Two Figures Need Verification Before Use

Spotify for Creators reports 69,044 plays and an audience of 49,649 all time, against 3,296 plays and an audience of 931 in March — a 21× and 53× increase in five months.

This does not reconcile with the same dashboard's own daily chart, which shows roughly 20–70 plays per day through July and August, nor with Buzzsprout attributing only 15% of downloads to Spotify. Spotify's notice on the page states that on 11 June 2026 it changed how plays are counted, which may explain part of it — though a stricter 30-second threshold should lower counts, not raise them.

Until this is checked on the Audience tab, these figures should not be quoted externally or used in funding reports. Nothing else in this brief depends on them. Spotify's follower count was not in the export supplied and remains unknown; it stood at 252 in March.

One Spotify figure is usable: the dashboard reports a normal range of 24–28 plays in an episode's first seven days, which is consistent with the daily chart and with Buzzsprout's Spotify share.

Six

Diagnosing the Data

Six things the numbers show. Each follows from figures above; none extends into recommendation.

1. Reach and subscriber growth come from different places

The channel's view count is a Shorts number and its subscriber count is an episode number. 85 Shorts delivered 53 subscribers; episode 1.1 alone delivered 122. Any metric that blends the two — total views, channel-wide CTR, channel-wide view duration — describes neither format and moves mainly with the ratio between them. This is why March's 1% conversion figure was misleading: arithmetically correct, but averaging two populations that differ by a factor of 23.

Measured separately, full episodes convert at 2.35%, which sits near the low end of the 3–5% range typical for niche educational channels rather than far below it.

2. Click-through improved, retention did not

Season 2 lifts mean CTR from 4.33% to 5.49% and median from 4.22% to 5.51% — consistent across both measures, so not an artefact of one outlier. Over the same rewrite, mean retention moved from 21.7% to 19.9% and median from 22.1% to 20.9%: flat, marginally down.

The repackaging changed behaviour at the thumbnail and did not change behaviour after the click. Roughly 80% of every episode goes unwatched, and that is true of Season 2 as much as Season 1.

3. Episode 2.5 is an outlier in two directions at once

2.5 "Who Created the Creator?" has the most views of any Season 2 episode (1,306), the most subscribers (34), and the worst retention on the entire channel — 11.3%, an average view of 6:45 against a 60-minute episode. It drew 21,085 impressions, more than any episode except 1.1.

High reach with unusually shallow engagement is the signature of traffic arriving from outside the established audience. In the July–August window it supplied 30% of all views and 37% of all subscribers; remove it and the period is close to flat. The channel's recent growth rests substantially on one episode whose viewers mostly did not stay.

4. The back catalogue is decaying on video and holding on audio

Across the seven-week window, 14 Canto 1 episodes drew fewer than 50 views each. Episodes 1.14, 1.5 and 1.12 drew 8, 11 and 11 respectively. Yet those same early episodes dominate audio downloads, with 1.5 second overall at 418.

The archive is functioning as a sequential course for audio listeners and as dead stock on YouTube. The two platforms are not degrading together — only YouTube is.

5. Impressions nearly doubled and per-episode click-through rose with them

Impressions grew 91% while episode-level CTR improved between seasons. Growing impressions usually dilute CTR, because the additional impressions are shown to progressively less relevant viewers. Here both moved up. That combination indicates YouTube is distributing the channel more widely and the packaging is holding up under wider distribution — the most straightforwardly positive pattern in this dataset.

6. Retention varies enough to rule out format as the cause

Retention across 26 episodes ranges from 11.3% to 31.3% — nearly a threefold spread, on episodes of near-identical length, structure and production. Episode 1.5 holds viewers for 17:56 and 1.8 for 17:31; 2.5 holds them for 6:45.

If the hour-long conversational format were itself the ceiling, that spread would be narrow. It is not. Whatever drives retention here varies episode to episode — which the data locates in the content and its packaging rather than in the format.
